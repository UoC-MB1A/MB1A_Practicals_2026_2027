    Part 5: Conditional Probability and Bayes’ Theorem

    TipLearning objectives

    By the end of this part of the practical you will be able to:

    • Calculate conditional probabilities from observed data in R.
    • Calculate and interpret the sensitivity and specificity of a biological marker in R.
    • Use simulation to investigate conditional probability and Bayes’ theorem.
    • Use simulation to estimate the probability of an outcome given observed evidence.
    • Explain how the prevalence of an outcome affects the interpretation of a marker or test result.

    UK cervical screening data

    In the UK, cervical screening is routinely offered to any individual with a cervix aged 25 to 64. This screening tests for high risk strains of human papillomavirus (HPV). Members of this group of viruses can damage cells in the cervix and cause cervical cancer. Where tests are positive for high risk HPV (hrHPV), the samples are further tested for abnormal cells and, if abnormal cells are found, additional tests for pre-cancerous cells and cervical cancer are performed.

    Therefore, testing for hrHPV can be considered to be a marker for abnormal cervical cells, and therefore for pre-cancerous conditions of the cervix and cervical cancer.

    The following data are from this publication, published in 2006 in the British Journal of Cancer (note that this is before routine vaccination against HPV was introduced in the UK. In this study 24,510 people in the UK were screened for hrHPV and cytology was performed in all cases to detect abnormal cells. In this practical we will consider 21,843 of these people - excluding people who had borderline or mild abnormal cytology.

    Of these 21,843, 12.1% of people tested positive for hrHPV. 2.1% of people had moderately or severely abnormal cytology, the remainder were normal.

    The number of people with and without hrHPV and with and without abnormal cytology are provided in CSV format in data/cm_all_ages.csv.

    Calculate the proportion of people with abnormal cells who have hrHPV - the sensitivity of the hrHPV as a marker.

    \[ \text{Sensitivity} = \frac{\text{True positives}} {\text{True positives} + \text{False negatives}} \]

    # Count how many people have abnormal cells -
# true positives and false negatives
ab_count <- tp + fn

# abnormal cells who also have hpv
prop_hpv_given_abnormal <- tp / ab_count
print(prop_hpv_given_abnormal)
    [1] 0.9006479

    The sensitivity of HPV status as a test for abnormal cells is 90.06%.

    Calculate the proportion of people who have normal cells and don’t have hrHPV - the specificity of hrHPV as a marker.

    \[ \text{Specificity} = \frac{\text{True negatives}} {\text{True negatives} + \text{False positives}} \]

    # Count how many people have normal cells -
# true negatives and false positives
n_count <- tn + fp

# Calculate the proportion of people who have
# normal cells who don't have hpv
prop_no_hpv_given_normal <- tn / n_count
print(prop_no_hpv_given_normal)
    [1] 0.895884

    The specificity of HPV status as a test for abnormal cells is 89.59%.

    So far, we’ve calculated proportions using the whole sample of 21,843 individuals. However, the prevalence of moderate/severe abnormal cytology varies considerably with age.

    In this sample the authors described:

    • 5.08% prevalence of moderate/severe abnormal cytology in individuals aged 20-29
    • 1.81% prevalence of moderate/severe abnormal cytology in individuals aged 30-49
    • 0.47% prevalence of moderate/severe abnormal cytology individuals aged 50-64

    AnswerAnswer

    For each of these age groups, simulate a sample of 10,000 individuals with the appropriate prevalence of moderate/severe cytology.

    For the individuals in your simulation with and without moderate/severe cytology, simulate the presence of HPV based on the frequencies calculated in exercise 1.

    In each iteration, store the number of people with HPV, the number of people with abnormal cells and the number of people with abnormal cells and HPV in a data frame.

    prevs <- c(0.0508, 0.0181, 0.0047)

n_sim <- 10000
results <- tibble()

for (prop_abnormal in prevs) {
  # Simulate a population of people with normal and
  # abnormal cells at the population rate
  # 1 = abnormal
  # 0 = normal
  cyt_statuses <- rbinom(n_sim, 1, prop_abnormal)

  # Count how many people have abnormal cells
  n_abnormal <- sum(cyt_statuses == 1)
  n_normal <- sum(cyt_statuses == 0)

  # For the people with abnormal cells, simulate HPV
  # presence based on the proportion of people with HPV
  # given abnormal cells calculated in ex1.

  hpv_statuses_abnormal <- rbinom(
    n_abnormal, 1,
    prop_hpv_given_abnormal
  )

  # For the people with normal cells, simulate HPV
  # presence

  # Calculate the proportion of people with HPV given
  # normal cells
  prop_hpv_given_normal <- 1 - prop_no_hpv_given_normal

  hpv_statuses_normal <- rbinom(
    n_normal, 1,
    prop_hpv_given_normal
  )

  # How many people have HPV
  n_hpv <- sum(hpv_statuses_normal == 1) + sum(hpv_statuses_abnormal == 1)

  # How many people have HPV and abnormal cells - true positives
  n_hpv_abnormal <- sum(hpv_statuses_abnormal == 1)

  # Generate a row for the data frame with these results
  current <- tibble(
    Prevalence = prop_abnormal,
    N_Abnormal = n_abnormal,
    N_HPV = n_hpv,
    N_TP = n_hpv_abnormal
  )

  # Add this result to the data frame
  results <- results |> bind_rows(current)
}

    Why does the probability that an individual in each group with hrHPV has moderate-or-worse abnormal cytology differ so much, even though the sensitivity and specificity of hrHPV as a marker were kept constant?

    Because the prevalence of abnormal cells changes so much between the groups. The sensitivity of the test tells us the probability the person will test positive if they have the disease and the specificity tells us the probability they will test negative if they don’t have the disease. The ratio of people who do and don’t have the disease differs between the three samples.

    NoteExtension exercise: Cervical cancer

    We can extend this simulation to consider cervical cancer as an outcome, rather than just abnormal cells.

    Based on this paper published in Cytopathology in 2016 and also based on UK individuals, the positive predictive value of moderate or severely abnormal cells for cervical cancer is:

    • 1.5% for people aged 20-29
    • 3.4% for people aged 30-49
    • 5.1% for people aged 50-64

    (Note that these are estimates, as this paper uses slightly different age ranges)

    This means that, based on this sample, for example, 1.5% of people aged 20-29 with at least moderately abnormal cells will then be diagnosed with cervical cancer. In this study no-one of any age with normal cells was diagnosed with cervical cancer.

    Extend your simulation to calculate the probability that a 24 year old who is HPV positive and has a cervix would be diagnosed with cervical cancer on further testing (assuming prevalence is constant across the 20-29 age group).

    AnswerAnswer 
    results_cc <- tibble()
prop_cc_given_abnormal <- 0.015
prop_abnormal <- 0.0508

# Simulate a population of people with normal and
# abnormal cells at the population rate for age 20-29
# 1 = abnormal
# 0 = normal
cyt_statuses <- rbinom(n_sim, 1, prop_abnormal)

# Count how many people have abnormal cells
n_abnormal <- sum(cyt_statuses == 1)
n_normal <- sum(cyt_statuses == 0)
  
# For the people with abnormal cells, simulate HPV
# presence based on the proportion of people with HPV
# given abnormal cells calculated in ex1.
hpv_statuses_abnormal <- rbinom(
  n_abnormal, 1,
  prop_hpv_given_abnormal
)

# For the people with normal cells, simulate HPV
# presence based on the proportion of people with HPV
# given abnormal cells calculated in ex1.
hpv_statuses_normal <- rbinom(
  n_normal, 1,
  prop_hpv_given_normal
)

# Only the abnormal cells people can have HPV in this example
# For the people with abnormal cells, simulate cervical
# cancer with a prevalence of 0.015
cc_statuses_abnormal <- rbinom(
  n_abnormal, 1,
  prop_cc_given_abnormal
)
# How many people have HPV
n_hpv <- sum(hpv_statuses_abnormal == 1) + sum(hpv_statuses_normal == 1)

# How many people have HPV and cervical cancer
n_hpv_cc <- sum(cc_statuses_abnormal == 1 & hpv_statuses_abnormal == 1)

# What proportion of people with HPV have cervical cancer
prop_cc <- n_hpv_cc / n_hpv

print(prop_cc)
    [1] 0.001385042

    Under this simplified model, we estimate that 0.14% of HPV-positive individuals aged 20–29 would be diagnosed with cervical cancer.

    Note

    This is a simplified model designed to explore conditional probability and should not be interpreted as a model of an individual’s clinical risk.
