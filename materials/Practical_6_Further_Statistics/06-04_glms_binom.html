    As you may have noticed, all of the statistical tests introduced in Practical 4 (except for chi-squared) expect a continuous response variable.

    A standard linear model assumes that the response variable is continuous, with observations normally distributed around an expected value. A linear combination of the explanatory variables determines this expected value.

    However, not all response variables are continuous or normally distributed. A generalised linear model (GLM) extends the idea of a linear model to response variables with other distributions.

    Binomial response variables

    In Part 2: Hypothesis Testing, we treated the presence or absence of a species as our explanatory variable when asking whether mean temperature differed between sites where a species was present and absent. We then used our model to predict mean temperature in the presence of this species in Part 6: Using models to make predictions.

    However, we may instead want to ask: does the probability of species presence change with temperature?

    In the case of H. tumulorum we would therefore have the following hypotheses:

    • Alternative: There is a relationship between minimum temperature in the coldest month and the probability of detecting H. tumulorum.
    • Null: There is no relationship between minimum temperature in the coldest month and the probability of detecting H. tumulorum.

    In this case, detection of H. tumulorum becomes our response variable and minimum temperature becomes our explanatory variable.

    Our response variable is binary: at each site H. tumulorum was either detected (1) or not detected (0). We can model this using a binomial distribution, where \(p\) represents the probability of detection. We therefore need our predicted values of \(p\) to lie between 0 and 1.

    Why not use a linear model?

    Let’s try just using a linear model. First, we need to load our H. tumulorum dataset. We should also use the as.integer() function to convert our logical (TRUE / FALSE) detected column into a numerical column with 1 for detected and 0 for not detected.

    Then we can apply our linear model, with minimum temperature as the explanatory variable and detection of H. tumulorum as the response.

    # Load data, convert detected to integers
ht_df <- read_csv("data/ht_df.csv") |>
  mutate(detected_01 = as.integer(detected))

# Apply linear model
lm_binary <- lm(detected_01 ~ min_temp_coldest_month, data = ht_df)

# Display linear model
summary(lm_binary)

    A linear model can predict any numerical value, whereas a probability must always lie between 0 and 1 (0–100%). We therefore need a model that retains the useful linear relationship with our explanatory variable, but converts its predictions onto a scale appropriate for a probability.

    Link functions in binomial GLMs

    Link functions connect the predictors in our model to the response variables in a linear way.

    In a binomial GLM, the logit link function transforms probabilities into log-odds.

    \[ \operatorname{logit}(p) = \ln\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right) \]

    ExerciseExercise 2

    In your prac6_exercises.qmd Quarto file, write a function to calculate the log-odds from a probability. In R log() is the natural logarithm.

    Use your function to calculate the log-odds for a sequence of probabiliies from 0 to 1, with increment 0.1. Observe what happens to the log-odds as the probability increases.

    # Write the function
calc_log_odds <- function(prob){
  odds <- prob / (1 - prob)
  log_odds <- log(odds)
  return (log_odds)
}

# Apply the function to the sequence
lo_vec <- c()
for (val in seq(0, 1, 0.1)){
  lo <- calc_log_odds(val)
  lo_vec <- c(lo_vec, lo)
}
print (lo_vec)
     [1]       -Inf -2.1972246 -1.3862944 -0.8472979 -0.4054651  0.0000000
 [7]  0.4054651  0.8472979  1.3862944  2.1972246        Inf

    There is an increase in log-odds as probability increases.

    The natural logarithm of the odds (log-odds) can take any value from \(-\infty\) to \(\infty\). We can therefore model the log-odds as a linear combination of our explanatory variables.

    Fitting binomial GLMs in R

    If we use a GLM instead of a linear model, it estimates the linear relationship between minimum temperature and presence / absence on the log-odds scale.

    We can apply a GLM in R glm() function. We use the argument family = binomial to specify that we want to predict a binomial response. Otherwise, the function is applied in the same way as a linear model, but with glm() instead of lm().

    ExerciseExercise 3

    We would report these findings as follows:

    There was strong evidence that minimum temperature in the coldest month was associated with the probability of detecting H. tumulorum (binomial GLM, \(\beta\) = 1.65, \(z\) = 3.87), \(p\) = 0.0001.     \(\beta\) = 1.65 tells us that for every 1°C increase in minimum temperature, the log-odds of detecting H. tumulorum increase by 1.65.

    So, for every 1°C increase in minimum temperature, the odds of detecting H. tumulorum are multiplied by approximately 5.2.

    As you can see, the prediction is limited to probabilities between 0 and 1.

    Making predictions

    ExerciseExercise 4

    In your glms.qmd Quarto file, use your GLM to predict the probability of detecting H. tumulorum at the following temperatures:

    • 0°C
    • 1°C
    • 2°C

    By default, predict() returns predictions from this model on the log-odds scale. The argument type = "response" applies the inverse link function and returns predictions on the original response scale — in this case, probabilities.

    AnswerAnswer 
    # Make a data frame of the temperatures for which
# we want to predict probabilities
df_unknown <- tibble(
  min_temp_coldest_month = c(0, 1, 2)
)

# Predict the probability of presence or absence at these
# temperatures
pred <- predict(
  glm_ht,
  newdata = df_unknown,
  type = "response"
)

# Put the results in a data frame
pred_df <- tibble(min_temp = c(0, 1, 2),
                  predicted_prob = pred)
gt(pred_df)
    min_temp predicted_prob
    0 0.2211643
    1 0.5973837
    2 0.8857500

    So, there is a 22.12% probability of seeing H. tumulorum with a minimum temperature of 0°C, a 59.74% probability at 1°C and an 88.57% probability at 2°C.

    Let’s go back to our extreme temperatures, -10°C and 20°C, and do the same thing again.

    df_extreme <- tibble(
  min_temp_coldest_month = c(-10, 20)
)

pred <- predict(
  glm_ht,
  newdata = df_extreme,
  type = "response"
)

pred_df <- tibble(min_temp = c(-10, 20),
                  predicted_prob = pred)
gt(pred_df)
    min_temp predicted_prob
    -10 1.872149e-08
    20 1.000000e+00

    These values, essentially 0 at -10°C and 1 at 20°C may still be incorrect, but they are at least valid probabilites. Our model describes the relationship supported by the observed data, which has a minimum temperature of -2.29°C and a maximum temperature of 3.49°C. There is no guarantee that the same relationship continues beyond this range. Making predictions outside the observed range is called extrapolation, and these predictions can be highly unreliable.

    For example, let’s predict the probability of finding H. tumulorum at 1000°C.

    df_extremer <- tibble(
  min_temp_coldest_month = 1000)

pred <- predict(
  glm_ht,
  newdata = df_extremer,
  type = "response"
)

pred_df <- tibble(min_temp = 1000,
                  predicted_prob = pred)
gt(pred_df)
    min_temp predicted_prob
    1000 1

    According to our model, we are guaranteed to find H. tumulorum at this temperature.

    Using the logit function forces the predicted probability to be between 0 and 1, however it doesn’t guarantee it is correct.

    Multiple predictors

    Additional predictors and interactions can be added to GLMs exactly as we added them to linear models in ?sec-p3_hypothesis_testing_2 and ?sec-p4_hypothesis_testing_3.

    ExerciseExercise 5

    In your glms.qmd Quarto file, apply a single binomial GLM to assess the following hypotheses, based on the data in data/site_metadata.csv.

    Annual mean temperature

    Null hypothesis: At the average latitude, the probability of Halictidae being the dominant insect family is not associated with annual mean temperature.

    Alternative hypothesis: At the average latitude, the probability of Halictidae being the dominant insect family is associated with annual mean temperature.

    Latitude

    Null hypothesis: At the average annual mean temperature, the probability of Halictidae being the dominant insect family is not associated with latitude.

    Alternative hypothesis: At the average annual mean temperature, the probability of Halictidae being the dominant insect family is associated with latitude.

    Interaction

    Null hypothesis: The relationship between annual mean temperature and the probability of Halictidae being the dominant insect family does not depend on latitude.

    Alternative hypothesis: The relationship between annual mean temperature and the probability of Halictidae being the dominant insect family depends on latitude.
